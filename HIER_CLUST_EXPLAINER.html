Hierarchical Clustering

Hierarchical clustering is an unsupervised technique that builds a tree of nested groupings instead of asking you to fix the number of clusters up front. This explainer focuses on the agglomerative ("bottom-up", AGNES) approach: how linkage methods measure cluster distance, how the dendrogram is built one merge at a time, and how to read the tree to decide on an optimal number of clusters.

Our working dataset — 14 customers by income & spending score
💡 Hierarchical clustering is unsupervised: there is no target label to predict, only a similarity measure (typically Euclidean distance on standardized variables) used to decide which observations belong together.
🌳 Unlike k-means, you don't choose k before running the algorithm. Agglomerative clustering builds the entire hierarchy — from 14 singleton clusters down to 1 root — and you decide afterward, by reading the tree, where to "cut" it.
🎯 This explainer focuses on the more commonly used agglomerative approach (AGNES). The same data and tree-cutting logic can be adapted to divisive clustering (DIANA), which works in the opposite direction.

⚠️ Single linkage tends to "chain" — long, straggly clusters can form because only the single closest pair matters. Complete linkage over-corrects toward tight, compact, similarly-sized clusters. Average and Ward's usually sit in a practical middle ground, which is why Ward's method is a common default.

✂️ The height of a branch shows how dissimilar the two things it joins are. A tall vertical jump just below the cut line means you are separating clusters that are genuinely distinct — that's a good place to cut. A cut through short, densely-packed branches means you're splitting up observations that are still quite similar.
